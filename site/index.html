Nine seconds of an engine that does not exist. Nothing in it is a sample: every pressure wave was computed, from a bore, a stroke, a camshaft and a crankshaft. Press play, then fit the other crankshaft while it runs.

The question

A Mustang burbles. A Ferrari shrieks. Both are 90° V8s, and I wanted to know why they sound nothing alike. Not well enough to describe it. Well enough to point at the part.

The usual answer is that the American engine fires unevenly. It does not. Both fire once every 90° of rotation, eight times a cycle, perfectly spaced. At the flywheel the two keep identical time.

What a manifold hears

An engine does not exhale through its flywheel. It exhales through two exhaust manifolds, and each one is bolted to four cylinders and hears only those four. Which of them fire when is decided by one part: the crankshaft, whose four throws, seen from the nose, make either a cross or a line.

Here are two cycles of it, slowed until they can be counted: the whole engine, and then what each manifold gets of it. Listen to the engine, then to one bank alone.

The engine ticks evenly. Alone, a bank coughs twice in quick succession, waits three quarters of a turn, and coughs again. That limp, in each pipe, out of step with the other, is the burble. Now fit the flat crank and listen to one bank again.

On the flat crank a bank alone is as even as the whole engine. Nothing limps, so nothing burbles. Same block, same heads, same cam.

What one tailpipe looks like

The picture at the top of the page is the recording from one tailpipe: time runs left to right, pitch runs upward to 500 Hz, and each rising line is a harmonic of the engine’s speed. Here are both cranks, computed in your browser from the same two files you have been listening to.

Nobody typed in the firing order

This is the whole crankshaft as the source describes it: a bank angle, four throws, and eight rods. It is what a foundry decides, and nothing more.

1-5-4-2-6-3-7-8 is cast into the intake manifold of every 302 Ford built in 1968. It is not an input here. Typing it in would have taken a second and ended the project, because a program that has been told the answer cannot surprise you. It is handed the forging, and the order falls out, and the limp with it, and the sound with that.

The rest is written the same way

The source is meant to be read the way a shop manual is. You give it what Ford’s drawings give, in the units Ford used, and a bore is a different type from a stroke, so the compiler checks the machinist’s work.

Behind that, each cylinder is an open thermodynamic system integrated in crank angle: crank-slider kinematics, Wiebe combustion, Woschni heat loss, compressible flow past the valves. The exhaust is a digital waveguide. Inside the engine everything is SI, and inches exist only where a person types them. It is header-only C++23 on the standard library and nothing else, and it should still build in fifteen years.

Is it the engine Ford sold

Close, and wrong in a way I can name: the curve is too broad, with torque peaking about 400 rpm low and power 600 high. The missing part is intake runner tuning, which I built three times and shipped none of, along with a nonlinear gas solver that was verified, measured and taken out. What came out, and why is written down, because a limitation somebody built and reverted is a result.

Why

To find out whether the burble falls out of the geometry or has to be put there by hand. It falls out. What that left behind is an engine that runs in a terminal and cannot move anything, and does so correctly. It is not useful. It is the most careful thing I have made.

A C++23 compiler and make. There is nothing else to install.
